Affirm has agreed to buy Ratepay from Nexi. The deal has been [announced on 9 November, but not closed yet](https://www.nexigroup.com/content/dam/corp/downloads/media/press-release/2026/2026-11-09-pr-Nexi-divests-Ratepay-and-plans-strategic-BNPL-partnership-with-Affirm.pdf): regulatory approval is still pending, with completion expected in the first half of 2027. Nexi called the sale a move towards its core business; Ratepay calls it a chance to combine its local invoice-payment expertise with Affirm’s scale and longer-term instalment products. So much for the press-speak, sounds sensible on paper. If you've been part of German fintech long enough, you probably know that this is anything but a victory lap, and the story of how a German BNPL pioneer ended up here is not really cheerful.

The digital ink wasn't even dry on this announcement and some people in this little corner of payments have already treated the news as an invitation to advertise their own BNPL products and platforms. Declaring themselves the real deal on LinkedIn posts and comments, the last man standing, accompanied by badly generated AI-slop market analysis plastered with competitor logos. Claude, do market research, make no mistakes. Apart from the bad taste it leaves in one's mouth: lads, maybe wait until the people losing their jobs with this announcement have made it home, will ya?

While never working at Ratepay, I collaborated and competed with so many people from Ratepay over the years in this so very niche corner of payments within the DACH market, I do feel like I had a front-row seat to the story of one of Germany’s most successful fintechs. And having built so many BNPL platforms and products over the years, I think I have a firm grasp on what went sideways, which I am trying to convey in this post without any gleeful cheer whatsoever.

Hindsight is 20/20, but it also shows that Ratepay kind of "had it all" and came from such a place of privilege, as one of the earliest players in the game. And while being handed such a great starting hand, they somehow fumbled this game along the way, to become the unwanted child of Nexi. As per usual, for anyone doubting this reflection, I brought plenty of receipts, since almost all of it is public record.

## From poster boy to unwanted son

[Miriam Wohlfarth co-founded Ratepay in 2009](https://www.miriam-wohlfarth.com/about-me/). By 2017, when Otto sold it to Advent and Bain for integration into Concardis, it already had [more than 100 employees and offered invoice, instalment, direct-debit and prepayment products](https://www.milbank.com/en/news/milbank-advises-otto-on-sale-of-ratepay.html). It became part of Nexi in 2021. Miriam herself said it had been profitable since 2016, and [Finanz-Szene reports that revenue rose by more than two-thirds to almost €100 million in 2021](https://finanz-szene.de/payments/einmal-unicorn-und-zurueck-so-drastisch-wurde-ratepay-von-nexi-abgewertet/). Truly, that pandemic e-commerce boom propelled BNPL providers to new heights, but the business success was established by the original founder and management team long before that. On a personal note, it was great to observe Ratepay do everything "right" and "by the book", long before the pandemic in those early years. They were one of the first BNPL players to be a BaFin regulated entity, having some of the youngest Managing Directors under BaFin supervision in its history. Trailblazers in so many ways.

Ratepay's roots and historic ties with Otto and its cloud of brands were - by all means - something of an unfair advantage to the rest of the market, who had to fight and establish a distribution and base scale, while Ratepay could simply ride and grow on the volume of the Otto group.

Klarna had to buy that scale in DACH with insane amounts of venture capital (or [by simply buying BillPay off the market](https://www.mynewsdesk.com/klarna/pressreleases/klarna-announces-acquisition-of-billpay-1786866). Remember BillPay, anyone?). Today's [Riverty](https://www.riverty.com/en/business/newsroom/riverty-starts-operations/) was simply living off of its [Arvato / Bertelsmann network](https://www.bertelsmann.com/en/divisions/arvato-group/), from logistics to marketing and all the other deep industry networks, which made its Enterprise focus possible in the first place. But that still required work.

Meanwhile, Ratepay was leading away all of them, despite having neither bathtubs of VC money nor deep enterprise networks to tap into. They simply had the volume in-house. And a starting leadership team knowing exactly what they wanted to achieve with it.

But somewhere, at the latest with the Nexi deal, weird news out of Berlin started to pile up, and from my front-row seat, looking back now, that must have been the first signal that things are going sideways. Just as Nexi themselves admitted in last week's press release, Ratepay was just not a good fit for the Italian-led payment giant. And just like the company wasn't a fit for this situation, the original leadership team had left the building by 2022: [Urs Bader, formerly COO, moved to Regis24 in 2019](https://www.regis24.de/menschen-bei-regis24-urs-bader-ceo/); [Jesper Wahrendorf stepped down as CEO in 2020](https://financefwd.com/de/ratepay-ceo/); and [founder Miriam Wohlfarth left in 2021](https://financefwd.com/de/ratepay-fuehrung-miriam-wohlfarth/). While writing this I am reminded what Eric Ries of Lean Startup fame has described in his recent book *Incorruptible* ([Marty Cagan's review](https://www.svpg.com/great-products-bad-companies/)), if you haven't read it, I strongly recommend it.

Some of the news in the following years was of the rather harmless but amusing kind, like the "White Label Hero" campaign from 2022: a woman dancing through Berlin and blending into different surroundings to represent an invisible payment provider. [The 52-second film](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fSe45K9XSek) went onto Ratepay's YouTube channel on 9 November. It even came with [a blog post dated 7 November 2022](https://web.archive.org/web/20260122051111/https://website-p01.ratepay.com/en/ratepay-insights/invisibility-that-can-be-seen-whats-behind-our-white-label-hero-campaign/) explaining it, and they spread it on all of their digital channels, LinkedIn, Youtube and certain display ads in trade publications. It was supposed to be "B2C-style content to B2B clients". Ok, I guess. Personally, I was just left mildly confused, since their positioning was that of a white-label pure player. It might have been rooted in a "pick-me" moment versus Klarna, who had already gone big on consumer-facing campaigns: [Snoop Dogg as "Smoooth Dogg" in January 2019](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/snoop-dogg-becomes-a-shareholder-in-klarna-and-transforms-into-smoooth-dogg/), [Maya Rudolph in a US Super Bowl ad in February 2021](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-makes-its-big-game-debut-with-the-four-quarter-sized-cowboys-campaign-starring-four-singing-maya-rudolphs/), and [A$AP Rocky in a global campaign in June 2021](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/aap-rocky-and-klarna-team-up-for-global-campaign/). Klarna wanted you as the consumer to download their app, so the positioning checks out. Ratepay was undecided if they should leave even their logo in a merchant's checkout or not.

Maybe wasting marketing budget was also rooted in the valuation story that came out earlier that same year. [Bloomberg reported in February 2022](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/mergers-and-acquisitions/nexi-said-to-weigh-sale-of-1-1-billion-buy-now-pay-later-unit) that Nexi was considering a sale at a possible valuation above €1 billion. That was a reported *potential* valuation, not a transaction. [No buyer emerged at the time](https://finanz-szene.de/payments/einmal-unicorn-und-zurueck-so-drastisch-wurde-ratepay-von-nexi-abgewertet/). No quick flip for Nexi, but that was definitely news-worthy and of course a reason to hang yourself with all the unicorn bling. It was one of [at least two attempts by Nexi to sell Ratepay](https://finanz-szene.de/payments/nexi-wollte-ratepay-nur-noch-loswerden-aber-was-will-affirm-jetzt-mit-ratepay/) to the highest bidder in the past years.

Ultimately, inflated valuations or wasted marketing budget are not any bad news per se. But when customers start to wander off, you are in trouble. And wander off they did, oh boy.

[Finance Forward reported in 2023](https://financefwd.com/de/ratepay-kaeufersuche/) that Otto (of all customers!) had moved payments to its own platform in 2022 and About You (one of Ratepay's formerly biggest customers) had shifted most of its BNPL volume to Klarna by that point. The effect on Ratepay's revenue was substantial. Losing big merchants is a problem when your cost base, risk operation and product organisation were built around that scale, and assumed as integral part of your company basically since its inception. But now Ratepay had to survive the pricing war in a race to the bottom that Klarna accelerated, since their income share has been shifting more and more towards the consumer side, away from the merchant service fee. But merchant fees are your *main* revenue source as a white-label infrastructure player, and when merchant fees collapse, you get squeezed.

There were also rumours, sometimes confirmed reports, of them struggling with their scale and growth, running on a legacy infrastructure. While I don't want to entertain industry rumours, as early as November 2021 there were [Handelsblatt reports](https://www.handelsblatt.com/finanzen/banken-versicherungen/banken/onlinezahlungen-ebay-setzt-wegen-moeglichen-betrugs-zahlungen-per-lastschrift-aus-/27822500.html) that eBay had suspended Ratepay-powered direct debit in Germany since late October after reports of unauthorised purchases and payment demands. Both companies confirmed the suspension. Ratepay said it was investigating possible identity theft; eBay said it was working with Ratepay on a fix. [OnlinehändlerNews published eBay's account of the reported transactions](https://ohn.haendlerbund.de/themen/marktplaetze/135645-ebay-lastschrift-zahlungen-indentitiaetsdiebstahl). Now, let me tell you completely unfiltered: SEPA direct debit, as a bastardised pay later payment method, sucks. Just plainly sucks. SEPA rails were not made for that and with Germans' aversion to any access-2-account innovation, it will still take years until this is made feasible. But it just shows that issues were real, both on the risk and revenue side.

Weird product development and sunset decisions were another aspect, which is purely my own reading as someone who has run various white-label BNPL platforms. That started with them entertaining [pre-payment (Vorkasse)](https://www.milbank.com/en/news/milbank-advises-otto-on-sale-of-ratepay.html) as a BNPL player - you get the oxymoron (I know everyone dreamed of the de-risking large transactions with down-payments of the first rate, but no one made it work in DACH without credit cards as the payment method). Then at some point, already towards the mid 2020s, they stopped marketing their instalment offering, could be due to unmitigated BaFin pressure, or in anxious anticipation of what [CCD2](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=oj%3AL_202302225) is bringing - I can't tell. Beneficiaries were competitors, including my former venture, grabbing one of the most profitable shares of their volume. The 24+ month instalment product was replaced with the rather tame [pay-in-3](https://docs.ratepay.com/docs/about_ratepay/faq/products), which, by all accounts, has almost no impact on your P&L. So we are left with the German-typical "Kauf auf Rechnung", which only really shines at massive scale and if you have any up-sell or cross-sell potential with the consumers picking it up at checkout. Which you don't, because you are not a consumer brand or [neo-bank crab](https://terminalvelocity.blog/posts/the-carcinisation-of-fintech/), milking the consumer for good revenues.

So while they had legacy issues with their platform, big names leaving for the shiny offerings of Klarna and Co, their product and platform strategy could not lead them out of that cul-de-sac.

Their spend, however, was a mismatch to this situation. Take this example: In May 2022, Ratepay [leased 4,300 square metres in Berlin-Kreuzberg](https://www.cushmanwakefield.com/en/germany/news/2022/05/deal-berlin-kreuzberg) for a headquarters it opened in [early 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/nina-p%C3%BCtz_ratepay-payments-team-activity-7033697696875958272-p9vA). The [interior-design firm describes workspaces across four floors](https://www.smow.com/works/references/ratepay-berlin). The [2023 annual accounts, reproduced in full here](https://handelsregister.ai/de/organizations/berlin/beteiligungsgesellschaften/ratepay-gmbh-93bb8dbc74fa372141ff23c456534892#report-1), put rent, leasing and ancillary rental costs together at €2.103 million, up from €712,000 in 2022. They also describe a ten-year lease with six rent-free months whose benefit is spread across the lease term. Either way, that is a striking commitment, just as the company's economics were changing, and probably half the staff stayed home, used to WFH during covid and while hybrid work setups were emerging everywhere.

Speaking of economics, register-data services [report a €24.83 million net loss for 2023 and €23.74 million for 2024](https://handelsregister.ai/de/organizations/berlin/beteiligungsgesellschaften/ratepay-gmbh-93bb8dbc74fa372141ff23c456534892), after a roughly €4.63 million profit in 2022. [Finanz-Szene also reported](https://finanz-szene.de/payments/duerre-ertraege-hoher-verlust-ratepay-verharrt-im-restrukturierungs-modus/) a write-down of almost €350 million by Nexi on Ratepay.

We could now also dive into the ever increasing chokehold of BaFin in the recent years, especially [after Wirecard](https://www.bundesfinanzministerium.de/Content/DE/Standardartikel/Themen/Internationales_Finanzmarkt/2021-02-02-dossier-wirecard.html), [the fine Ratepay got for inadequate anti-money-laundering compliance](https://www.it-finanzmagazin.de/bafin-verhaengt-bussgeld-gegen-ratepay-wegen-verstosses-gegen-zahlungsdiensteaufsichtsgesetz-223678/), etc., but at this point I feel this is absolutely unnecessary.

Quoting the famous philosopher Mike Tyson: everyone's gotta plan until they get punched in the face. Looking back, Ratepay and its second- and third-generation leadership team with their 2nd and 3rd hand owners were surely riding both the covid e-commerce boom as well as the inflated valuations that fintechs were carrying before them until AI took their lunch money and the new regulatory landscape kept punching them in the face - to a certain degree some of the decisions were understandable in this context, but the pivots were either wrong or late, probably due to living comfortably off of Nexi's balance sheet.

And this comfortable life has reached its end with the Affirm announcement.

## The usual human cost of a deal

[Ratepay CEO Sabrina Flunkert wrote](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/sabrina-flunkert_today-we-shared-important-news-nexi-group-activity-7525527510520840192-OD2G) that the company's current setup was not sustainable, that it was reorganising, and that some colleagues would leave. She took responsibility and invited prospective employers to contact Ratepay about them. [Finanz-Szene says Nexi confirmed these are layoffs](https://finanz-szene.de/payments/berliner-buy-now-pay-later-spezialist-ratepay-geht-an-affirm/); the number has not been disclosed. The same report puts current staffing at roughly 200, down from about 300 at the peak. None of this establishes that Affirm ordered the layoffs, nor does it tell us what the eventual combined organisation will look like.

Affirm is not buying only an interesting story about German fintech history; they surely did more thorough due diligence than I did for this blog post. Ratepay brings a merchant footprint, local payment habits and relationships in a market where [invoice payment remains a central part of the proposed combination](https://www.nexigroup.com/content/dam/corp/downloads/media/press-release/2026/2026-11-09-pr-Nexi-divests-Ratepay-and-plans-strategic-BNPL-partnership-with-Affirm.pdf). Affirm brings technology, underwriting and scale. How much of Ratepay's own platform and identity survives is unclear. The announcement explicitly envisages [Nexi providing transitional technology and operational support after closing](https://www.nexigroup.com/content/dam/corp/downloads/media/press-release/2026/2026-11-09-pr-Nexi-divests-Ratepay-and-plans-strategic-BNPL-partnership-with-Affirm.pdf), which will help but is surely also rooted in appeasing the regulators to accept this deal.

However this deal unfolds in the coming years, my bets are that Ratepay will change substantially, both in the technical and organisational setup.

## So, what's the point of all of this?

You can see where the market is headed without declaring BNPL dead. [PayPal sells pay-later alongside its checkout and wallet](https://www.paypal.com/de/business/accept-payments/checkout). [Klarna has begun rolling out its debit-first card in several European markets, with Germany planned for later](https://www.klarna.com/international/press/klarna-launches-debit-first-card-across-europe-for-everyday-spending/). These are different businesses, but they are making the same bet: paying later is more defensible as a feature of a broader payment or financial relationship than as your central product pitch and positioning. A standalone player can still win, but only with massive scale, distribution and risk discipline to earn its place at checkout (aka, be cheap enough to compete with Klarna). That is my reading of the market, not a law of nature. If you need it as a short sentence: BNPL is a feature, not a product (anymore). And a commodity, at the same time.

Affirm can surely help here, with deep pockets, but also deep expertise - probably most famously navigating extremely strict regulatory frameworks - I'd argue this is probably Affirm's greatest solved problem on the US market. While [half of Europe lost their bloody minds over CCD2](https://terminalvelocity.blog/posts/ccd2-power-and-politics/) - as a Product Manager who had to launch an instalment product in the US I can tell you: you have not lived until you fit a promissory note into a consumer checkout. If you know, you know. And navigated 50 different tax regimes, one for each state. Good luck, soldier.

Ratepay helped make this BNPL category normal in Germany, growing in its early days in the Otto corporation, which [established pay-by-invoice long before anyone knew we would be shopping via the Internet one day](https://www.ottogroup.com/de/ueber-uns/historie-und-gruender.php). It survived a burst BNPL and e-commerce bubble, lost major customers, and found a buyer with the resources to offer it another chapter. That deserves neither a victory lap nor a funeral speech.

I hope it's clear from this reflection that I don't see this outcome for Ratepay with any "Schadenfreude". Quite the opposite, I can very much empathise with the challenges the company has faced, and maybe, just maybe having a shareholder (again) that is truly aligned with ones business can lead to new heights. It can be assumed that the transition will be a tough one, with the usual hunt for synergies that come with such mergers. Maybe also with some large scale technical migrations, to be able to utilise Affirms underwriting-muscles to the fullest. None of that will be easy, but maybe worth it for Germany's BNPL poster child.

All that is left to say is good fortune for those who remain to manage this transition and good luck with the job hunt for those that had to be let go with this announcement.
